How to sleep in a noisy apartment

You cannot make an apartment silent, but you can make night noise easier to live with. The steps that usually help most are a better setup in the room, earplugs if they suit you, a calm conversation with a neighbor when that is fair, and a steady background sound so sudden noises stand out less.

This is a practical guide, not a medical one. It does not treat sleep disorders, and it is not a promise that you will sleep through everything. Some nights will still be noisy. The aim is to stack a few ordinary changes that often take the edge off.

Why apartment noise feels worse at night

Apartment noise is harder at night because the building is quieter, so each sound stands out, and because you are trying to rest rather than simply ignore it. Voices through a wall, a television, footsteps, a door in the hallway, traffic and a snoring partner are the usual culprits.

You do not need a perfect diagnosis of every sound. It does help to notice whether the noise is mid-level and fairly constant (talking, a TV, a road), sudden and sharp (doors, heels, a dropped pan), or deep bass that you feel as much as hear. The first two often respond to earplugs, soft furnishings and sound masking. Bass and structural thumps usually do not, and those are the cases where talking to a neighbor or a landlord matters more than any app or pair of plugs.

If the main problem is the person next door, there is also a shorter page on how to sleep with noisy neighbors using nature sounds. The rest of this guide covers the whole apartment: the room, earplugs, the conversation, nature sounds and a simple routine.

Change the room before you buy anything

Small changes in the room often help as much as a new gadget, and you can try them the same evening. Move the bed a little away from the noisiest shared wall or from a window that faces a road, put a rug on a bare floor, and close the gap under the door if hallway noise is the issue.

A bookcase or wardrobe against a shared wall is not soundproofing, but it can take a little of the edge off voices. Heavy curtains help more with street noise than with a neighbor through plasterboard, because they work on the window, not the wall. If two rooms are an option, sleep in the quieter one, even if it is not the official bedroom. A folded towel or a simple draft stopper at the bottom of the door is often enough to soften footsteps and voices from the corridor.

None of this will stop a subwoofer. It can, though, make ordinary apartment life a bit softer, which is the realistic goal.

Earplugs, and when they help

Earplugs are often the single most effective tool for voices, television and a snoring partner, because they sit at your ear and do not depend on a speaker. Foam plugs are cheap and work well if you roll them thinly and let them expand in the ear; wax or silicone plugs suit some people who dislike the deep foam seal.

Comfort matters more than the highest advertised rating, because a plug you cannot stand at 2 a.m. will end up on the bedside table. Give yourself a few nights to get used to them. If they hurt, try another shape or a shallower fit rather than forcing them. This guide cannot tell you what is right for your ears; if you feel pain, pressure or worry, stop and speak to a professional.

You can use earplugs on their own, or together with a quiet background sound at a low volume. The sound then only has to cover what still comes through, so you do not need to turn a speaker up.

Talking to a neighbor

A short, calm conversation in the daytime is usually better than a note under the door at midnight, and far better than banging on the wall. Say what you hear, when you hear it, and what would help — for example, shoes off after ten, or the subwoofer down a little — and give the other person a chance to explain. Many neighbors do not realise how much a wooden floor or a shared wall carries.

If the building has house rules or a quiet-hours clause, it is fair to mention them without turning the chat into a legal argument. Keep a simple log of dates and times if the problem continues, because a landlord or a housing manager will find that more useful than a general complaint. Escalation is for repeated, unreasonable noise after you have tried a civil approach, not for a one-off party.

If you are already angry when the noise is happening, wait until the next day if you safely can. A hallway confrontation at 1 a.m. rarely improves the week that follows. This is not a substitute for official advice in your country or your building. It is only a practical order of steps: ask politely first, then use the channels your building already has.

Sound masking with nature sounds

Sound masking means adding a steady background so that a sudden voice, a door or a snore is a smaller change in a quiet room. It is not noise cancelling, and it will not remove bass. A continuous nature sound — heavy rain, a stream, or ocean waves — is what many people use for this, because it has fewer gaps than a fire with long pauses between cracks.

Place the sound close to you: a small speaker on your side of the bed, or headphones if you both find that comfortable. Keep the volume only a little above the disturbance. Turning it all the way up is tiring, and it still will not fix a subwoofer. People often compare this to white noise; nature sounds are not the same thing, but they can mask everyday noise in a softer way.

Rain is the most common starting point, especially rain that lives on the phone and does not depend on a late-night video. There is a guide to offline rain sounds for sleeping, and a separate page if you specifically want rain on a roof sleep sounds. A crackling fireplace sound for sleep can be pleasant in a room that is already fairly quiet, but it usually masks less than a steady rain. A simple routine for noisy nights

A short, repeatable routine will not silence the building, but it stops you starting the night already on edge. Pick a regular window for going to bed, dim the lights, put earplugs in if you use them, start a familiar sound at a modest volume, and put the phone face down.

If you wake in the night, avoid checking messages. Turn the sound down or up a little if you need to, or reset a sleep timer, and give yourself a few minutes without treating every door in the hallway as a fresh emergency. Some nights will still be poor. The routine is there so that a bad hour does not become a week of improvising at 3 a.m.

Questions people often ask

Will nature sounds block a neighbor’s bass or subwoofer?

No. Masking works best on mid-level sounds such as voices, a television or traffic. Deep bass travels through the structure of the building, and earplugs and speakers rarely deal with it well. That is a talking-to-the-neighbor problem, or a landlord problem, not an app problem.

Can I use earplugs and a rain sound together?

Yes. Many people do. Keep the playback volume low, since the plugs already reduce what you hear, and use a steady sound such as rain rather than something with sudden cracks.

Is it better to play sounds from the phone or a speaker?

A source close to your ear usually works better than a phone on the far side of the room, because the masking sound is closer than the noise you are trying to cover. A small speaker on your bedside table is enough for most people.

Should I go next door when the noise is happening?

If it is late and you are already angry, it is usually wiser to wait until the next day. A short daytime conversation is easier for everyone to hear clearly, and it is less likely to turn into a hallway argument.

What if I also have a snoring partner, not just neighbors?

Use the same tools — earplugs, a sound close to your side of the bed, and a conversation by day — and read the shorter guide on sleep sounds to drown out a snoring partner. An app cannot stop someone snoring, and it is not a reason to put off advice from a health professional if you are worried about the snoring itself.
